The Renters' Rights Act 2025 has been the biggest change to the private rented sector in a generation, and as of this month the transitional period is genuinely over. If you have been waiting to see how it settles before acting, that window has closed.
{: .lead-in}

Here is where things stand, and what is coming next.

## What changed on 1 May 2026

The bulk of the Act commenced in a single "big bang" on 1 May. From that date:

- **Section 21 was abolished.** No-fault evictions are gone. Possession now requires a valid Section 8 notice relying on one of the statutory grounds, some mandatory, some discretionary.
- **Fixed terms ended.** Existing assured shorthold tenancies converted automatically to assured periodic tenancies. New fixed-term ASTs cannot be created.
- **Tenants can leave on two months' notice** from day one of a tenancy, unless a shorter period is agreed.
- **Written statements of terms** became mandatory for new tenancies, and must be provided before the agreement is signed.
- **Awaab's Law reached the private sector**, bringing fixed timescales for responding to serious hazards.

There was also a one-off duty to send an official information sheet to every tenant on an existing written tenancy, with a deadline of 31 May 2026 and penalties of up to £7,000 for missing it. If that passed you by, take advice quickly rather than quietly.

## The last Section 21 notices have now lapsed

This is the part worth checking today. Landlords who had served a valid Section 21 notice before commencement were given a limited runway: if you had not asked the court to issue the claim form by **31 July 2026**, the notice lapsed and the tenancy is now assured periodic.

In practice that means there is no longer any live Section 21 anywhere in the system. If you were relying on an old notice to recover a property, you will need to start again under Section 8 and evidence a ground.

## What's coming: the PRS Database

Phase two begins with the Private Rented Sector Database, which the government has said will start rolling out from late 2026, region by region. Registration will be mandatory for all private landlords, with an annual fee confirmed closer to launch.

What you will be asked for is the interesting bit. The published roadmap points to landlord contact details, property information, and core safety records: **gas, electrical and EPC certificates**.

If that list looks familiar, it should. It is more or less the evidence pack a well-run letting should already hold. Landlords who keep certificates current and findable will register in an afternoon. Landlords with a folder of scanned PDFs in three different email accounts will not.

The PRS Landlord Ombudsman follows the Database rather than arriving alongside it, with mandatory membership expected around 2028.

## What to do between now and then

1. **Audit your certificates.** Gas Safety, EPC, EICR and insurance, with the expiry dates written down somewhere you will actually look. Reminders beat renewals discovered during an inspection.
2. **Check your tenancy paperwork.** Written statements of terms for anything new, and a clear record of what was provided and when.
3. **Get your possession grounds straight.** If you may need a property back, understand which Section 8 ground applies and what evidence it requires, well before you need it.
4. **Tidy your records now, not in December.** Registration will be far less painful if the underlying documents are already in order.

## Where this leaves council lettings

One knock-on effect worth naming: as compliance obligations rise, the gap between casually managed lettings and properly run ones widens. Local Authority placements sit firmly at the run-properly end. Certificates are checked before a property is ever seen by a housing team, the paperwork is generated rather than improvised, and the household is placed by an organisation with its own statutory duties.

For landlords already doing the work, that is less of a burden and more of an advantage.
