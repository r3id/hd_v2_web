Most compliance problems are not caused by landlords cutting corners. They are caused by a certificate quietly expiring while everyone assumes someone else is watching the date. Here is the full set, what each one actually covers, and the deadlines that matter.
{: .lead-in}

## 1. Gas Safety Record (CP12)

**Valid for: 12 months**

If the property has any gas appliances, pipework or flues, they must be checked every year by a Gas Safe registered engineer. The certificate you receive is properly called a Gas Safety Record, though almost everyone still says CP12.

The deadlines around the copy are where landlords most often trip up:

- A copy must reach existing tenants **within 28 days** of the check.
- New tenants must receive a copy **before they move in**.
- You must keep records for **at least two years**.

A useful quirk: you can have the check done up to two months before the current record expires without losing the original anniversary date. That means you can book early to fit an engineer's diary without the renewal date slowly drifting later each year.

This is the one with the sharpest consequences. Gas offences carry unlimited fines and, in serious cases, imprisonment, and letting with an expired record can invalidate your insurance at exactly the moment you need it.

## 2. Electrical Installation Condition Report (EICR)

**Valid for: 5 years, or sooner if the report says so**

Since the Electrical Safety Standards in the Private Rented Sector (England) Regulations 2020, every private tenancy needs a valid EICR carried out by a qualified person. It inspects the fixed installation: consumer unit, circuits, sockets, and permanent fittings, rather than the kettle.

The report codes each observation, and the codes decide what happens next:

- **C1 (danger present)** and **C2 (potentially dangerous)** mean the report is unsatisfactory. Remedial work must be completed **within 28 days**, or sooner if the report specifies, and you must obtain written confirmation.
- **FI** means further investigation is required, and is also treated as unsatisfactory.
- **C3** is advisory. Worth doing, but it does not make the report fail.

Copies go to existing tenants **within 28 days**, to new tenants **before occupation**, and to the local authority **within 7 days** if they ask. Penalties reach £30,000 per breach.

Note the "or sooner" in the validity. Some reports specify a shorter interval, and that shorter date is the one that counts, not the default five years.

## 3. Energy Performance Certificate (EPC)

**Valid for: 10 years, but the minimum standard is changing**

An EPC lasts a decade from issue, and you need a valid one before marketing a property. Simple enough. The complication is the minimum rating you are allowed to let at.

Right now that minimum is **EPC E** under the Minimum Energy Efficiency Standards. But on 21 January 2026, as part of the Warm Homes Plan, the government confirmed the domestic private rented minimum will rise to the equivalent of **EPC C, with a single compliance deadline of 1 October 2030** for all tenancies in England and Wales.

Detail worth knowing now rather than in 2029:

- The **cost cap rises to £10,000** per property, up from £3,500, and the maximum penalty rises to **£30,000** per property per breach.
- Spending from **1 October 2025 onwards** counts towards the cost cap, so improvement work you do today is not wasted.
- Properties that reach C under the current methodology **before 1 October 2029** are treated as compliant until that EPC expires.
- The approach is fabric-first: insulation and draughtproofing, then a choice between improving the heating system or meeting smart-readiness standards.
- Exemptions exist, but they must be registered on the PRS Exemptions Register. An unregistered exemption is not an exemption.

The legislation itself is expected in 2027, so specifics may shift. The direction will not.

## 4. Landlord insurance

**Valid for: usually 12 months**

Not a statutory requirement in the way the others are, but in practice non-negotiable: standard residential cover does not extend to a let property, and any Local Authority placing a household will expect to see appropriate landlord insurance in place.

Two things to check rather than assume. First, that the policy covers the letting type you actually have, since HMOs, student lets and council placements are treated differently by insurers. Second, that your compliance is current, because insurers routinely decline claims where a gas or electrical certificate had lapsed at the time of the incident. The certificates and the cover are not separate concerns.

## 5. Fire safety: risk assessment and alarms

**Valid for: reviewed regularly, and after any material change**

This is less a single certificate than a bundle, which is precisely why it gets missed.

If you let an HMO, or a building with common parts, you need a **fire risk assessment** under the Regulatory Reform (Fire Safety) Order 2005. There is no fixed expiry date. It must be reviewed regularly and whenever something material changes: a layout alteration, a change of use, new occupants, or a near miss.

Alongside it, the alarm requirements apply to every rented home in England:

- A **smoke alarm on every storey** used as living accommodation.
- A **carbon monoxide alarm in every room** with a fixed combustion appliance, excluding gas cookers.
- Alarms **tested and confirmed working on the first day** of a new tenancy.
- Faulty alarms **repaired or replaced** once reported.

HMOs carry more besides, including fire doors, emergency lighting and escape routes, all typically covered by licence conditions.

## The dates in one place

- **Gas Safety Record**: every 12 months. Tenant copy within 28 days, new tenants before move-in.
- **EICR**: every 5 years, or sooner if specified. C1, C2 or FI means remedial work within 28 days.
- **EPC**: every 10 years. Minimum E now, equivalent of C from 1 October 2030.
- **Insurance**: usually annual. Check it covers your actual letting type.
- **Fire risk assessment**: no fixed expiry. Review regularly and after any material change.

## Why this is about to matter more

Phase two of the Renters' Rights Act brings the Private Rented Sector Database, rolling out from late 2026. Registration will be mandatory, and the published roadmap indicates landlords will need to supply property information and core safety records: gas, electrical and EPC.

In other words, the documents above stop being something you produce when challenged and become something you register centrally. Landlords whose certificates are current and findable will register quickly. Landlords whose paperwork lives across three email accounts and a drawer will not enjoy the process.

Getting the tracking sorted this year is considerably cheaper than getting it sorted under a deadline.
