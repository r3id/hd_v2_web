The latest statutory homelessness statistics make for hard reading, and for anyone working in a housing options team they will not be a surprise. What is worth pulling apart is not the headline figure, but the composition underneath it.
{: .lead-in}

## The numbers

For January to March 2026, temporary accommodation in England reached a record high.

{% include stats.html items="135,580::Households in temporary accommodation, Jan to Mar 2026|177,530::Children living in temporary accommodation|63.8%::Of those households include children" %}

The cost side has risen just as sharply. Councils in England spent **£2.84 billion** on temporary accommodation in 2024/25, with net expenditure of £1.43 billion once subsidies and household contributions are accounted for. That headline figure was up around 25% in twelve months, and has roughly doubled over five years.

## Where the money actually goes

The breakdown matters more than the total. Of that spend, £844 million went on emergency B&Bs and hostels, widely regarded as the least suitable option for families. More than £1 billion went on nightly-paid self-contained accommodation, an increase of 79% in a single year.

Nightly-paid is the line to watch. It is the most expensive way to house a household, it is procured under time pressure, and it grows fastest precisely when teams have least capacity to shop around. The cost is not really a housing cost. It is the price of not having a suitable property visible at the moment the decision has to be made.

{% include pull.html text="Nightly-paid accommodation is what a placement costs when you can't see the alternative in time." %}

## Out of area, and what it signals

Out-of-area placements tell a similar story about supply rather than intent. At the end of June 2025, 81% of all out-of-area arrangements were households that had applied to London authorities. In London, more than 34,000 households were placed outside their home borough during 2025, up more than 40% in two years.

No housing officer wants to place a family away from their school, their GP and their support network. It happens when nothing suitable is visible nearby, within the time available. Which is, again, a visibility problem as much as a supply one.

## What can actually shift

Some of this needs national solutions that no platform can provide: more social housing, and Local Housing Allowance rates that track real local rents. Those arguments are being made ably by the LGA, CIH, Shelter and others, and they matter more than anything in this paragraph.

But part of the pressure is mechanical, and mechanical problems can be worked on now:

- **Suitable private stock exists and isn't visible.** Compliant, accredited landlords in most areas would work with a council and have no clear route in. Every one of those is a property not competing with a nightly-paid booking.
- **Speed determines cost.** The difference between placing a household on day two and day nine is often the difference between a leased property and an emergency booking. Search time is money in a very literal sense.
- **Evidence takes longer than the decision.** Reconstructing what was considered, offered and approved is a significant admin load. Recording it automatically as it happens costs nothing extra.

None of that fixes the supply shortfall. It does mean fewer households in the most expensive and least suitable accommodation while the shortfall is being addressed, which is worth having.
